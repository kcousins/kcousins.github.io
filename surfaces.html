The only exit large enough

Three excretory-organ surfaces, drawn to scale on a field where every square is one square metre.

Lay the body's great internal surfaces on the same hundred-square-metre field and the contest is over before it begins. The lungs fill it — roughly 100 m² of alveolar membrane, about half a singles tennis court. The entire gastrointestinal tract, microvilli and all, occupies barely 30 m² (√30 ≈ 5.5 cells a side — its edge falls between the fifth and sixth gridline). The kidney's filtering surface is a single cell: about 1 m². Count the cells; the arithmetic checks itself.

A fair fight needs the same caveat twice. First, these surfaces do different jobs — the gut absorbs, the kidney filters, only the lung exchanges gas — so the comparison is of scale, not of like with like. Second, and more deeply: area alone is necessary but not sufficient. The lung wins as carbon's exit not merely because it is largest, but because it is the only large surface meeting a continually refreshed gas phase — the atmosphere, an effectively infinite sink — across which a volatile molecule like CO₂ can simply leave. The skin, our other air interface, manages perhaps 2 m² and almost no gas exchange. (Lung area is variously cited at 50–130 m²; 100 is a round, slightly generous estimate chosen to make the grid count cleanly. The qualitative verdict holds across the whole range.)
